What is Kana?

01 / The Basics

Hiragana (ひらがな) is one of three writing systems used in the Japanese language. It consists of 46 basic characters, each representing a syllable — a vowel sound like "a" or a consonant-vowel pair like "ka" or "shi."

Unlike the English alphabet, where letters represent individual consonants or vowels, hiragana characters represent whole syllables. This makes it a syllabary rather than an alphabet. Once you learn the 46 sounds, you can read and pronounce any hiragana text.

要点 · Key Concept

Each hiragana character = one syllable = one sound. There are no silent letters, no irregular pronunciations. What you see is exactly what you say.

03 / A Brief History

Hiragana has a fascinating origin — it evolved from simplified Chinese characters over more than a thousand years.

5th–6th Century
Chinese characters (kanji) arrive in Japan through Korea and China. Japanese scholars begin using kanji to write Japanese, sometimes for meaning, sometimes purely for their sounds.
8th Century — Man'yogana
A system emerges where kanji are used solely for their phonetic value, ignoring their original meaning. For example, the kanji 安 ("peace," pronounced "an") is used just for the sound "a." This system is called Man'yogana.
9th Century — Cursive Simplification
Court women, who had limited access to formal Chinese education, developed a flowing, cursive style of writing Man'yogana. Over time, the characters became more and more simplified. 安 became あ. 以 became い. The result: hiragana.
10th–11th Century — Literary Flourishing
Hiragana becomes the script of Japan's great literary works. The Tale of Genji and The Pillow Book are written largely in hiragana, establishing it as a vehicle for artistic expression.
1900 — Standardization
The Japanese government standardizes the hiragana set to 46 characters, selecting one character form for each syllable from the many variants in use.
豆知識 · Fun Fact

Hiragana was historically called "women's hand" (女手, onnade) because of its origins among court women. Ironically, it went on to become the most fundamental script in Japanese — learned first by every child and student.

04 / How Hiragana Works

The 46 hiragana characters are organized into a grid called the gojuon (fifty-sound chart), arranged by consonant rows and vowel columns:

Row Characters Sounds
Vowels あ い う え お a, i, u, e, o
K-row か き く け こ ka, ki, ku, ke, ko
S-row さ し す せ そ sa, shi, su, se, so
T-row た ち つ て と ta, chi, tsu, te, to
N-row な に ぬ ね の na, ni, nu, ne, no
H-row は ひ ふ へ ほ ha, hi, fu, he, ho
M-row ま み む め も ma, mi, mu, me, mo
Y-row や ゆ よ ya, yu, yo
R-row ら り る れ ろ ra, ri, ru, re, ro
W + N わ を ん wa, wo, n

Each row follows the same vowel order: a, i, u, e, o. So once you know the vowels, you just pair them with each consonant. "K + a = か," "K + i = き," and so on.

Modifying Sounds: Dakuten & Handakuten

While there are 46 foundational characters, you can expand the variety of sounds you can make using two types of small diacritical marks added to the top-right corner of specific characters: dakuten (゛) and handakuten (゜).

Dakuten (゛): Often called tenten ("dots"), this mark transforms unvoiced (soft) consonant sounds into voiced (hard) sounds. It modifies four specific character rows:

• K-row → G-row: か (ka) becomes が (ga)

• S-row → Z-row: さ (sa) becomes ざ (za) (Note: し/shi becomes じ/ji)

• T-row → D-row: た (ta) becomes だ (da) (Note: ち/chi becomes ぢ/ji, つ/tsu becomes づ/zu)

• H-row → B-row: は (ha) becomes ば (ba)

ha + dakuten (゛) = ba
"ha" turns into the voiced "ba" sound

Handakuten (゜): Often called maru ("circle"), this mark is exclusively used with the H-row. It transforms the breathy "H" sound into a crisp, plosive "P" sound.

• H-row → P-row: は (ha) becomes ぱ (pa), ひ (hi) becomes ぴ (pi), and so on.

Printed Style — 明朝体 (Mincho)

This is the hiragana you'll see in textbooks, on screens, and in newspapers. The characters have even stroke widths and sharp, well-defined shapes. Think of it as the "Times New Roman" of Japanese. It's the best reference when you're first learning to recognize each character.

Handwritten Style — 手書き (Tegaki)

When Japanese people write by hand, the characters become rounder and more flowing. Strokes sometimes connect where they don't in print, and proportions shift slightly. For example, handwritten さ (sa) often connects the top horizontal stroke to the curved body, while the printed version keeps them separate.

助言 · Tip for Learners

Don't worry if your handwritten hiragana doesn't look exactly like the printed version. In Japan, schools teach a handwriting font called "kyokasho-tai" (textbook style) that bridges the gap between print and natural handwriting. Focus on stroke order, and the flow will come naturally.

Calligraphic Style — 書道 (Shodo)

Japanese calligraphy (shodo, literally "the way of writing") transforms hiragana into art. Written with a brush and ink on paper or silk, the strokes vary dramatically in thickness — thin where the brush lifts, thick where it presses. The beauty lies in the balance between control and spontaneity.

Calligraphic hiragana is closest to its historical roots. Since hiragana evolved from cursive Chinese characters, brush calligraphy reveals the flowing, connected nature that the printed form hides. It's both a practical art and a meditative practice in Japan.

06 / Why Learn Hiragana First?

If you're just starting your Japanese journey, hiragana is the best place to begin. Here's why:

It's the phonetic base. Every sound in Japanese can be written in hiragana. Even complex kanji have hiragana readings. Master these 46 characters and you can read the pronunciation of anything.

It's everywhere. Children's books, furigana reading guides, grammar particles, verb conjugations — hiragana appears on every page of Japanese text.

It's achievable. Most learners can recognize all 46 characters within one to two weeks with regular practice. Unlike kanji (which has thousands of characters), hiragana is a finite, conquerable goal.

It unlocks everything else. Katakana mirrors hiragana structurally (same sounds, different shapes), so learning one makes the other much easier. And kanji readings are given in hiragana, so you need it to study kanji effectively.

07 / What is Katakana?

Katakana (カタカナ) is the second kana script. Like hiragana, it has 46 basic characters representing exactly the same set of syllables. The sounds are identical — only the shapes differ.

While hiragana characters have rounded, flowing strokes derived from cursive Chinese writing, katakana characters are angular and sharp, derived from parts of Chinese characters rather than their cursive forms.

違い · Key Difference

Hiragana and Katakana represent the same sounds but are used in different contexts. Think of them like lowercase and uppercase letters — same language, different visual roles.

Origin of Katakana

Katakana was developed by Buddhist monks during the 9th century. While court women simplified whole Chinese characters into the flowing hiragana, monks took a different approach — they extracted individual components from Chinese characters to create shorthand annotations for reading Chinese texts.

For example, the katakana ア (a) comes from the left side of the kanji 阿. The katakana カ (ka) comes from the left side of 加. This extraction method gave katakana its distinctive angular, fragmented appearance.

08 / When is Katakana Used?

In modern Japanese, katakana has several specific roles:

Foreign loanwords: This is the most common use. Words borrowed from English and other languages are written in katakana. コーヒー (koohii, "coffee"), テレビ (terebi, "TV"), パン (pan, "bread" from Portuguese).

Foreign names: Names of people and places from other countries are written in katakana. アメリカ (Amerika), ロンドン (Rondon, "London"), マイク (Maiku, "Mike").

Onomatopoeia: Sound effects and mimetic words are often written in katakana for emphasis. ドキドキ (dokidoki, heartbeat), ピカピカ (pikapika, sparkling), ゴロゴロ (gorogoro, rumbling).

Emphasis: Similar to using italics or bold in English, katakana can highlight words for emphasis or stylistic effect.

Scientific and technical terms: Animal and plant names in scientific contexts, as well as some technical terminology, are written in katakana.

豆知識 · Fun Fact

Japan absorbs thousands of English words each year, writing them in katakana. Some get shortened in creative ways: パソコン (pasokon) from "personal computer," スマホ (sumaho) from "smartphone," and コンビニ (konbini) from "convenience store."

学習法 · Learning Strategy

Start with hiragana — it's used more frequently and forms the grammatical backbone of Japanese. Once you're comfortable, katakana will feel like learning a second font for the same sounds.
